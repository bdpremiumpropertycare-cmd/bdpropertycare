Why Pure Water Window Cleaning Outperforms Tap Water & Detergent

Reviewed against UK Health and Safety Executive guidance and manufacturer/technical sources on water purification. Last updated 2026.

Anyone who has cleaned a window with tap water and a cloth knows the frustration: the glass looks clean while wet, then dries to a hazy, streaked finish. This isn't a technique problem — it's a water chemistry problem, and understanding it explains why professional window cleaners have largely moved away from soap-and-squeegee methods toward pure water systems.

The Science: What's Actually in Tap Water

Ordinary mains water contains dissolved minerals, primarily calcium and magnesium, measured as Total Dissolved Solids (TDS). These minerals are invisible in the water itself, but as water evaporates from a glass surface, the minerals it was carrying are left behind as a fine residue — the streaks and spots you see once the glass dries.

Purified water — treated through deionisation resin or reverse osmosis to reach close to 0 parts per million TDS — behaves differently. Because pure water has no dissolved minerals of its own, it actively draws in and holds surrounding dirt and mineral particles from the glass to restore its own chemical equilibrium. When it evaporates, it leaves nothing behind, because there was nothing dissolved in it to leave.

How Pure Water (Water-Fed Pole) Systems Work

  • Filtration: mains water passes through a purification unit (deionisation, reverse osmosis, or both) that strips out dissolved minerals before it ever reaches the glass.
  • Agitation: a soft brush head on the end of a telescopic pole loosens dirt, pollen and grime from the glass surface.
  • Rinse and dry: the purified water rinses the loosened dirt away and is left to air-dry — because it carries no minerals, it dries completely clear, without a squeegee pass required.

This is the mechanism used across residential and commercial window cleaning today, and it's why no detergent is required — the water itself does the cleaning work.

The Safety Case for Ground-Level, Pole-Fed Cleaning

There's a second reason this method has become standard, separate from water chemistry: safety. The UK Health and Safety Executive is explicit that work at height should be avoided wherever reasonably practicable, specifically citing telescopic water-fed poles as a way to clean upper-floor windows without a ladder. HSE guidance on falls from height records that falls remain a leading cause of major workplace injury, with most serious injuries in this category linked to falls of under two metres — often from ladders used incorrectly, through overreaching or unstable positioning.

Extendable pole systems used at ground level remove that risk entirely for low-rise residential and commercial glazing, while still reaching windows that would otherwise require a ladder.

What This Means for Your Property

For homeowners and businesses, the practical takeaway is straightforward: a pure water system delivers a genuinely streak-free finish because of water chemistry, not just technique, and it does so more safely than ladder-based cleaning for accessible upper floors. It also means no detergent runoff into gardens, patios or drains.
