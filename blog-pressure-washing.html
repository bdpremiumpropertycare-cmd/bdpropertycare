Pressure Washing Driveways & Patios: Getting the Pressure and Runoff Right

Reviewed against Environmental Protection Agency (Ireland) and UK/US stormwater management guidance. Last updated 2026.

Pressure washing is one of the most effective ways to restore a driveway, patio or exterior surface — but "effective" depends entirely on matching the pressure and technique to the surface, and managing what happens to the water once it's done its job. Both of these are areas where doing it properly looks different from simply pointing a wand at the ground.

Why Pressure Selection Matters

Different surfaces tolerate very different levels of force. Dense materials like natural stone, brick paving and sealed concrete can typically handle higher pressure, while softer or more porous surfaces — older mortar joints, painted render, wood, and some block paving finishes — can be etched, pitted or stripped of their surface layer by excessive pressure. The same principle that governs roof cleaning applies more broadly: matching pressure to material prevents surface damage that can be difficult or costly to reverse, particularly on jointed paving where aggressive cleaning can wash out the sand or mortar between blocks and destabilise the surface over time.

This is why a competent pressure washing job starts with an assessment of the surface material and its condition, not a single default setting applied to every job.

The Runoff Question

The part of pressure washing that's easy to overlook is where the water goes once it leaves the surface. Pressure washing runoff carries whatever it removed — dirt, moss, oils, and any cleaning solution used — and if that water runs untreated into a storm drain, it typically flows directly into local rivers or coastal water without any treatment in between.

This is a recognised environmental concern, not a minor technicality. Stormwater drainage systems are typically built to carry rainwater straight to rivers, lakes or the sea without any treatment step in between — unlike a sink or toilet, which drains to a wastewater treatment works. In Ireland, Uisce Éireann (Irish Water) and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) are the relevant authorities for wastewater discharge standards and water quality, and the same underlying principle applies here: surface water drains carry rainwater and runoff directly to rivers and the coast, so uncontrolled discharge of contaminated wash water is a genuine environmental issue rather than a cosmetic one.

Why This Matters When Choosing Who Does The Work

This is part of why pressure washing is worth treating as a skilled job rather than a commodity service. Two operators can point the same equipment at the same driveway and get very different results depending on whether they've matched the pressure to the surface and thought about where the water is going — one leaves a clean surface with no lasting damage, the other can etch a surface, wash out paving joints, or send dirty runoff straight down a nearby drain.

The Practical Upshot

A driveway or patio that's been pressure washed properly should look dramatically better without any etching, pitting, or destabilised jointing. Getting that result consistently comes down to treating pressure washing as a technical job with a right and wrong way to do it, not just "spray until it looks clean."
